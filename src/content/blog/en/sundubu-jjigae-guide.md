**Sundubu jjigae (순두부찌개)** is Korean soft tofu stew. The tofu gives the dish its name, but it does not tell you everything in the pot. Before choosing a version, look at the full menu name and ask about the broth and additions.

## What makes the tofu different?

Sundubu is tofu in its delicate, coagulated form before the water is pressed out. The Korea Tourism Organization's [soft tofu stew introduction](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?vcontsId=178354) describes it cooked together with seafood. Think soft curds rather than the firm tofu cubes you may recognize from other dishes.

That helps distinguish the dish from two other menu names: kimchi jjigae centers on kimchi, while doenjang jjigae takes its name from fermented soybean paste. Our [kimchi and doenjang stew comparison](/blog/kimchi-jjigae-vs-doenjang-jjigae/) explains those differences.

## Read the words around “sundubu”

A more specific name can tell you which version the restaurant is offering. These examples are useful starting points, not complete ingredient lists.

- **바지락순두부찌개 — bajirak sundubu jjigae:** clam and soft tofu stew. VISITKOREA's [clam version](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?vcontsId=181138) describes chili oil seasoning and notes that shrimp, squid, or other seafood may also be used.
- **짬뽕순두부 — jjamppong sundubu:** a version combining soft tofu with spicy seafood soup. It appears in the tourism organization's [Chodang soft tofu guide](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?vcontsId=221275).

If the menu simply says 순두부찌개, there is still more to ask. Point to that exact entry rather than assuming it matches a version you tried elsewhere.

## Tofu does not mean a meat-free broth

The Chodang guide also describes a restaurant's spicy sundubu made with pork and beef-bone broth. That is a specific example of why the tofu in the name does not establish whether the whole dish is vegetarian.

Ask about the liquid and seasoning as well as the visible ingredients. Removing a piece of seafood or meat does not answer what was used to make the broth. If you avoid an ingredient, explain which one and ask staff to confirm the recipe they use. A reference description cannot verify your restaurant's preparation.

## Egg is another detail to check

An egg can be part of the meal. For example, the official [Maetdol Sundubu restaurant listing](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/whereToGo/locIntrdn/rgnContentsView.do?vcontsId=189249) describes a shrimp-and-clam version with an optional egg addition.

Treat that as an example, not a promise that every restaurant handles eggs the same way. Ask whether the egg is included, optional, or already added before requesting a change.

## Does sundubu always mean a spicy stew?

The ingredient and the finished dish are different things. VISITKOREA's Chodang guide describes plain soft tofu as well as spicy stews and seafood soup variations. Seeing “sundubu” alone does not tell you which preparation you are ordering.

Look for the complete name and ask about the heat level. If you prefer less chili, check whether the kitchen can adjust this particular dish before placing the order.

## Four questions before you order

1. Which ingredients are in this version, including the broth?
2. Does it come with an egg, and is the egg optional?
3. How spicy is it, and can the seasoning be adjusted?
4. Does the listed price include rice?

Keep the Korean menu name beside those questions so staff can identify the dish you mean. For help preparing that conversation, read [how to check ingredient information before ordering](/blog/check-ingredient-information-kbap/).
