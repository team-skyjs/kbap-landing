A Korean menu may list several dishes translated as “soup,” even though their names end differently: **국 (guk)**, **탕 (tang)**, or **찌개 (jjigae)**. Those endings offer a useful starting point. The rest of the name often tells you more about which dish you are choosing.

They are not three rigid categories. In its [explanation of these food terms](https://www.korean.go.kr/front/onlineQna/onlineQnaView.do?mn_id=216&qna_seq=309925), the National Institute of Korean Language says their meanings do not have completely clear boundaries and that customary usage matters.

## Use the ending to get your bearings

| Korean ending | A useful English starting point | What to remember |
|---|---|---|
| 국 — guk | Soup | Ingredients are cooked with plenty of water or broth. |
| 탕 — tang | Soup | Closely overlaps with guk; often associated with longer simmering. |
| 찌개 — jjigae | Stew | Generally has less broth than guk, with ingredients and seasoning concentrated in a smaller amount of liquid. |

The language institute describes **tang** as a more elevated term for guk, commonly used for a deeply simmered soup. That does not make “tang” a dependable measure of a restaurant's cooking time or quality. Likewise, “jjigae” suggests a stew, not a fixed number of ingredients or a particular portion size.

## Read the full name: miyeokguk and galbitang

In **미역국 (miyeokguk)**, *miyeok* refers to seaweed. The Korea Tourism Organization's [seaweed soup entry](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?vcontsId=178170) describes a version with beef broth and notes coastal variations containing clams or fish. Recognizing “seaweed soup” therefore does not give you the complete broth or ingredient list.

In **갈비탕 (galbitang)**, the dish is a beef rib soup. KTO's [galbitang description](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?vcontsId=182500) explains simmering beef ribs and bones with aromatics; glass noodles can also be added. The rib component distinguishes this dish far more usefully than trying to decide whether its English name should be “soup” or “stew.”

These are reference descriptions. Keep any extra words on the restaurant's menu in view, and ask about its own version rather than assuming every bowl follows the same recipe.

## Why the distinction is not always neat

The Korean Food Promotion Institute's [discussion of soup names](https://www.hansik.or.kr/magazines/list/magazineDetail/20/2888?menuSn=429) points out the overlap directly: *tang* functions like “soup” in **seolleongtang**, but more like “stew” in **maeuntang**. A single ending cannot do all the work of explaining a dish.

For jjigae, start with the word before it. **Kimchi jjigae** and **doenjang jjigae** share an ending but have different seasoning bases. Our [kimchi and soybean paste stew comparison](/blog/kimchi-jjigae-vs-doenjang-jjigae/) explains those two dishes without treating either as one universal recipe.

## Make the name useful when ordering

Once you recognize the ending, read the complete menu line and its description. Then clarify what matters for your meal:

- What is the broth made from, and what else is in this version?
- Is this an individual serving or a pot intended to share?
- Does the listed order include rice?

These are questions for the restaurant, not answers encoded in *guk*, *tang*, or *jjigae*. For quantity labels such as **인분**, see our [Korean menu portions guide](/blog/korean-menu-portions-sizes-extras/).
